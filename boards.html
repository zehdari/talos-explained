The vehicle · Chapter 3

The circuit boards

The team designs and solders almost every board inside Talos itself. Each one has a single job, most have their own small computer, and those show up in ROS as a handful of topics. This chapter goes through them board by board.

The short version
  • Five custom board designs sit in the board stack and camera cage (POAC, two ESC boards, camera cage breakout, LED, IVC), plus two add-on boards on the Orin and two boards in each battery housing.
  • Every board that runs firmware has its own RP2040 microcontroller and talks to the rest only over CAN. A micro-ROS agent on the Orin turns every board on the internal bus into ordinary ROS topics.
  • The POAC is the hub: both batteries in, 15 V, 5 V and 12 V out, and thruster power only while both the physical kill switch and its RP2040 allow it.
  • Boards are designed in Altium (repo electric_boogaloo), made by JLCPCB and soldered by the team.
  • The pictures are renders of the current designs; a board in the robot can be older.

The boards do the electrical jobs a computer can't do directly: switching tens of amps of thruster power, turning 18 V into 5 V, timing a motor signal to the microsecond. Talos splits those jobs across several small boards, so a fault stays on one board, and that board can be pulled, fixed and reflashed on its own. Power and wiring follows the power through the robot; Firmware on the boards covers the code.

How the boards connect

The internal CAN bus is one cable chain: from the LED board through the IVC board, both ESC boards and the POAC to the camera cage breakout, then to the Orin HAT and into the Orin as can0 (per the team's 2026 TDR drawing, Fig. 15; no harness design records the order). The battery housings sit on a second, slower bus. Almost all power starts at the POAC.

Two kinds of traffic share the internal bus:

  • micro-ROS. One agent on the Orin, copro_agent, bridges every board's micro-ROS client into ROS (copro_agent.launch.py runs micro_ros_agent can -D can0 -v4 -m rmw). The topics land under /talos (ROBOT_NAMESPACE): state/kill in this chapter is /talos/state/kill.
  • Raw thruster frames. Thrust skips ROS: mpc_controller writes one 8-byte frame per ESC board per tick, four signed 16-bit RPMs (send_thruster_cmd_canbus.c), read by the ESC firmware (ESCBoard/src/main.c).

The agent for the external bus (can1) is commented out in the same file. That is on purpose: the title of the commit of 27 Oct 2026 ends "… turn off external uros agent until poac/batteries happy" (720bdd9), so for now the battery housings' state/battery doesn't reach ROS. Computers and networks covers the Orin side of CAN.

One microcontroller on every board

Why one per board? The story is in the comments of DeviceList.jsonc:

  • The first-generation electronics, Mark 1 (in Talos's predecessor, Tempest), kept running into trouble with I²C, a simple two-wire chip-to-chip bus meant for short distances.
  • At a pool test in fall 2021 a 3-foot I²C run to the depth sensor stopped working. A spare Raspberry Pi Pico, reading the sensor and sending depth over USB, got the test going again.
  • The first Mark 2 plan shared one I²C bus between every board. That test killed it: Mark 2 put an RP2040 on every board and joined them with CAN, which is built for long cables in electrically noisy places.

Every board with a microcontroller copies the same Altium sheet, RP2040_with_CAN.SchDot, so the firmware shares one pin map and one CAN driver. The block's parts carry an R or C suffix: on every board U1R is the RP2040 and U1C the CAN controller.

Microcontroller
RP2040 (two Cortex-M0+ cores), 12 MHz crystal, W25Q128JV 16 MB flash. Builds assume 2 MB of flash.
CAN
An MCP2517FD CAN controller, which the RP2040 talks to over SPI (a fast chip-to-chip bus), and a TJA1442AT transceiver, which turns its logic levels into the bus's two-wire signal
CAN clock
The RP2040 makes 4 MHz on GPIO21 from its 48 MHz USB clock (driver). The MCP2517FD multiplies that by 10 to 40 MHz; SPI runs at 20 MHz (driver).
Termination
A slide switch (silk TERM ON) adds a split 120 Ω terminator (2 × 60.4 Ω). The firmware can read the switch's position.
Debug
Tag-Connect TC2030 pads for SWD (the chip's debug and flashing port), BOOT and RESET buttons, micro-USB, a debug serial output on GPIO16
Status LED
RGB, GPIO 13/14/15 (below)
Buses
Internal: configured for CAN FD, the newer CAN that can send a frame's data faster than its header (1 Mbit/s header, 5 Mbit/s data; robots/talos.h). In practice every frame on Talos today is classic CAN at 1 Mbit/s, at most 8 bytes: the firmware has FD switched off ("TODO: Enable CAN FD") and the Orin brings can0 up as classic (configure_jetpack.bash; see CAN and CANmore). External: classic CAN, 250 kbit/s.

A CAN bus needs a 120 Ω terminator at each end and nowhere else: a resistor across its two wires that stops signals bouncing back off the end of the cable. If the TDR's chain order is right, the ends are the LED board and the Orin HAT's can0 transceiver, and both have termination switches. The designs don't record the switch positions, so check the physical switches.

The POAC: batteries, rails and the kill switch

The POAC ("power + actuator", 177.8 × 127 mm) replaced the Mark 2 power and actuator boards to free space in the stack, and kept the power board's CAN ID 1. It does five jobs:

  • Combines the batteries. Each pack (Anderson PP45, J3 and J4) goes through two P-channel FETs (transistors used as power switches) onto one bus, V+. An LTC4412 ideal-diode controller switches them so they pass current one way only, like a diode without a diode's voltage drop. The higher pack supplies the robot and neither can feed the other (Two packs, one bus).
  • Makes the rails. Two TDK-Lambda i6A4W modules (20 A, 9–53 V in) make 15 V (PS1) and 5 V (PS2). A MIC29300 linear regulator makes 12 V from 15 V for the servo only. Each RP2040 board makes its own 3.3 V with an LM1117, another linear regulator.
  • Switches thruster power through the kill switch circuit (below).
  • Measures itself. An MCP3428 16-bit ADC reads four rails onto state/electrical once a second; two field names mislead, see What the POAC measures. An SHT41 reads temperature and humidity.
  • Drives the actuators. One Hiwonder bus servo indexes two torpedoes and two markers over a 115200-baud serial line. It is half-duplex: one wire, and the two ends take turns to talk. The RP2040 makes it with its PIO, small programmable engines that can drive pins in any timing pattern (Torpedoes, dropper and claw). The claw firmware the 2026 TDR describes (several servos, positions kept across reboots) is on unmerged branches; on master the claw topics are named but never subscribed. Which build is flashed on Talos isn't recorded anywhere; its state/firmware version would tell.

The kill switch, in hardware

The kill switch has to stop the thrusters even if all software has crashed, so it acts on the power path directly. Three SiR167DP P-channel FETs in parallel (30 V, 60 A each) sit between V+ and MOTOR_PWR, the line to the ESC boards. Three TC4432 drivers drive their gates from one signal, MOTOR_EN, which passes two things in series:

  • the physical switch, which pulls its line to ground when it's inserted;
  • a DG448 analog switch, opened and closed by the RP2040's SOFTWARE_KILL pin. The firmware comment says it exists "to kill power to the ESCs even if the kill switch is inserted" (safety_interface.c).

Try it: thruster power needs both.

Three details make it fail safe:

  • A dead microcontroller means no thrust. A 10 kΩ resistor pulls SOFTWARE_KILL low, so with the RP2040 in reset, unflashed or crashed, the normally-open DG448 stays open and a 47 kΩ pull-up holds MOTOR_EN at V+: P-FETs off. The firmware also starts in the kill state.
  • The RP2040 sees the switch too. An N-channel FET translates the switch line to 3.3 V on GPIO1 (PHYS_KILLSWITCH), and the firmware treats it as kill slot 0, so pulling the switch opens both paths. The board publishes state/kill every 150 ms and on every change; the ESC boards stop their thrusters when they see it.
  • Software holds kill slots too, such as the Nereus viewer's Kill button. The slots and their timeouts are in Commanding Talos.

The ESC boards

Each ESC board (177.8 × 127 mm, the POAC's outline) carries four APD 80F3 electronic speed controllers (ESCs), the power stages that spin brushless motors. The two boards are identical. A solder jumper, SB1, tells them apart: left open, the board is ID 0 (thrusters 0–3, CAN client 2); bridged, it is ID 1 (thrusters 4–7, client 3). The firmware reads it once at boot (main.c).

The silkscreen labels ESC 1/5 … ESC 4/8 count from 1 (board 0 / board 1). The firmware, the MPC and state/thrusters/* count from 0. The pre-test thruster test names them VUS, VUP, HUS, HUP, HLS, HLP, VLS, VLP in that order (Thrusters and ESCs).

Power
MOTOR_PWR on Würth Redcube terminals, reverse-voltage P-FETs, a 60 A board fuse, a 15 A fuse per ESC
Motor signal
Bidirectional DShot600, a digital motor-command protocol, generated by the RP2040's PIO: one wire per ESC carries the command out and the RPM back. A serial telemetry line returns temperature, voltage, current and charge used.
Commands
Raw CAN frames from mpc_controller (four RPMs per frame), or command/thruster_rpm over micro-ROS
Safety
Thrusters stay off for 5 s after ESC power appears, and stop if no command arrives for 500 ms (then stay off for 1 s). ESC power counts as present above 9 V (dshot.h).
ESC firmware
Stock APD firmware had no reversible ("3D") DShot, so the team patched it in (README). Each ESC has a FLASH CONN header.

The camera cage breakout

The CCBB (50.8 × 136.6 mm) takes 15 V, 5 V and internal CAN from the POAC's CAMERA CAGE connector and hands them out in the camera cage:

  • Orin power: 15 V through a P-FET switch, on at boot. The viewer's "Cycle computer" sends CYCLE_COMPUTER on command/electrical, and the CCBB turns the Orin off for 5 s, then back on (ros.c). It also handles the fan, LED-strip and clear-depth commands (table).
  • DVL power over its Ethernet cable: J14 (DVL SUBCONN) adds 15 V on pins 7–8; J13 passes the data on to the network switch, which J16 powers at 5 V.
  • Depth sensor: an MS5837-02BA (the sensor in Blue Robotics' Bar02), published as state/depth/raw in frame talos/pressure_link. The long I²C run was the Mark 1 failure above; now the sensor plugs straight into the CCBB on a short cable (J15), and its depth reaches the Orin over CAN.
  • CAN to the Orin: J10 (ORIN CAN) carries both buses to the Orin HAT. J1 and J5 (EXT. CAN) carry the external bus and 15 V.
  • Cooling and lights: four 5 V fan outputs with tachometers, a switched 15 V Peltier output and an RGB status strip.

The firmware still creates a state/leak publisher, but its publish is commented out; the leak sensor is no longer used. The design doesn't show whether a Peltier and all four fans are fitted today.

Other buttons in the viewer's Electrical panel: Cycle IMU is handled on the Orin by imu_power_cycle.py (header pin 22 low for 10 s, through an Orin HAT switch the Sep 2026 design removes). No Talos firmware, on master or any branch, acts on Cycle acoustics or Kill acoustics. The same panel's Pinger section drives the IVC board (next).

The LED and IVC boards

The LED board drives the status light, an aluminium-core module with four RGBW LEDs and a thermistor:

  • Hardware: two ST L99LD20 buck LED drivers straight off V+, two colour channels each.
  • Commands: colour and mode (solid, slow or fast flash, breathing) from command/led. It flashes red when the kill switch is pulled and blue when it's inserted.
  • Self-protection: out of the water (depth above −5 cm on state/depth/raw) it runs at 1 % brightness. It dims past 30 °C and turns off at 50 °C (ledc_driver.c).
  • Revision: Talos has the 2023-24 ("Year One") board. A five-driver redesign is in the repo, but the firmware's pin map only fits the two-driver board.

The IVC board ("inter-vehicle communication", 2025) does two jobs with one hydrophone. It is an acoustic modem for talking to Triton, the team's second vehicle, and it is how Talos hears the competition pinger (2026 TDR).

  • Transmit: the RP2040 makes a tone, filtered (OPA1664) and amplified by an OPA548 power op-amp on ±24 V from an isolated TRACO converter, into a transducer the team built.
  • Receive: a hydrophone feeds a filter and amplifier chain on an isolated ±5 V supply; the RP2040's ADC samples it and an FFT finds the tones.
  • Protocol on master: 17 and 19 kHz tones, 50 ms per bit, 8 data bits and a 4-bit CRC (ivc.c). The 2026 TDR describes 51–53 kHz tones.
  • Pinger: the IVC firmware on the unmerged blake/ivc branch (IVCBoard2) also measures the hydrophone's amplitude at one chosen pinger frequency, 20 to 40 kHz (ivc.h), and streams it on ivc/pinger/selected_freq_amp_stream.
  • Pinger, Orin side: pinger_broker re-sends the chosen frequency to the board every second (30 kHz until told otherwise). riptide_acoustics compares how loud the pinger was in two listening windows. The mission tree's RandomPingerTree listens for 10 s near the torpedo board and 10 s near the table, then asks which was louder. The Pinger section of the viewer's Electrical panel turns listening on and picks 25, 30, 35, 37 or 40 kHz (panel config).
  • Which firmware is on the robot? As with the POAC, it isn't recorded: the board's state/firmware version tells you. The branch also renames some modem topics, so master's list may not be what's flashed.
  • Extra: a BM83 Bluetooth audio module can play sound through the transducer.

A solder jumper lets the same board serve another vehicle ("Populate SB5 for Lil' Boat, DNP for Talos"; LilBoatBoards holds the 2025 second vehicle's boards).

Inside the smart battery housings

Each pack is a MaxAmps 8000 mAh 5S2P LiPo, 18.5 V nominal, about 148 Wh. The team's battery notes set the working limits at 4.15 V per cell when charging and 3.2 V when discharging (BQ40Z80 notes). Each pack sits in a sealed housing with two boards. What the housing does for the pack is in What "smart" means; the boards:

  • BMS board: a TI BQ40Z80 battery manager watches and balances the five cells and controls two 80 V, 100 A N-channel protection FETs, backed by a 45 A Littelfuse protector.
  • MCU board: an RP2040 reads the BQ40Z80 over SMBus, a stricter form of I²C (in PIO, because the board's pins don't match the RP2040's I²C pin options), drives a small OLED and reports state/battery on the external bus. A reed switch is the power button.
  • Its CAN ID is its serial number (housings #1 to #5), not "port" or "starboard" (main.c). Its side comes from a side-detect pin instead, reported in the detect field of state/battery (ros.c); the viewer's PORT and STBD readings use it.
  • It can cut the robot's power: CYCLE_ROBOT briefly opens the FETs and KILL_ROBOT_POWER latches them open (ros.c). The viewer asks for confirmation before sending either.

Two boards on the Orin

Neither has a microcontroller; they give the Orin the ports it lacks. The 2026 TDR calls them the "Orin HAT" and the "FOG Board".

  • Orin HAT (Jetson AGX breakout, 52 × 34 mm) on the 40-pin header: two TJA1442 CAN transceivers for the Orin's logic-level CAN controllers (can0 internal, can1 external), each with a termination switch, and an SN65C3221 transceiver for the VectorNav IMU, which speaks RS-232, the classic serial-port signal, on /dev/ttyTHS1 (talos_launch_mpc.yaml).
  • FOG board (M.2 E-key card, 22 × 30 mm): an SP335E transceiver in RS-422 mode (serial over a pair of wires carrying opposite signals, which shrugs off noise) and a 3.3 → 8 → 5 V supply for the fibre-optic gyro on /dev/ttyTHS2 (gyro_config.yaml).

The sensors themselves are in Navigation sensors.

From idea to a board in the robot

Steps 1–3 follow the team's how-to, ProjectCreation.md; the rest is what the repo and its history show.

  1. Set up Altium once. Clone electric_boogaloo, import UWRTPreferences.DXPPrf, point Altium's document and library paths at the repo, and register UWRT_Template_Project as a template.
  2. Start from the template. New project from UWRT_Project_Template, sheets named ProjectName_SheetName.SchDoc; keep the 2- or 4-layer PCB template you need. Set ProjectTitle and TeamLead; leave BoardRevision at 1.0 and DateOfDesignReview until the review. On each sheet set Date, Author and Title for its title block.
  3. Use the team's libraries and blocks. Add the repo's Library folder as file-based libraries and take parts from there; the microcontroller comes from Device_Sheets/RP2040_with_CAN.SchDot, so the firmware's shared code just works.
  4. Lay it out for the fab. Use JLCPCB's 4-layer rules and stack-up from the repo (JLCPCB_4Layers.RUL, .stackup). Boards in one stack share an outline: POAC and ESC boards are both 177.8 × 127 mm.
  5. Design review. The date goes in DateOfDesignReview (the ESC board's reads 01/08/2023). Commits say when a design isn't ready: "ready for design review … do not order boards on this commit".
  6. Generate the order files. The output job runs the design-rule check and exports the BOM, Gerber X2 and drill files (set up in 2022 for "JLC export").
  7. Order from JLCPCB; the BOMs link parts to Digi-Key and Mouser. Each round's schematics go into a GitHub release: Tempest MK 2 (Jan 2023), 2023-24 Board Orders, 2024-2025 Board Orders.
  8. Assemble and test. The electrical team assembles and tests the boards itself, surface-mount soldering included (electrical team page).
  9. Bring it up. Flash the first firmware over micro-USB or the SWD pads, add the board's serial number to DeviceList.jsonc, and set the termination switch and any ID jumper. After that the bootloader takes updates over CAN (Firmware on the boards).

Mark 1, Mark 2 and the POAC

Most of Talos's electronics are older than Talos. The Mark 2 system (the "Tempest MK 2" order, January 2023) brought the RP2040-and-CAN design, and its ESC boards, camera cage breakout and battery boards moved over to Talos. The 2023-24 order added the POAC, the LED board and module, and the FOG board; the IVC board followed in 2025. The same orders hold boards that aren't in Talos today (below).

The renders may be newer than the robot

These designs changed after their boards were probably built: POAC (Feb 2026, separate 12 V servo output), ESC board (late 2025, voltage protector), battery BMS (Feb 2026, current-sense fix), Orin HAT (Sep 2026, IMU power switch removed), camera cage breakout (Nov 2024, connectors and linear regulators; unconfirmed whether rebuilt). Check the physical board.

When a board misbehaves

  • Look at the status LED first (decoder above). Dark: no power, or CAN never started. Red: check the CAN cable and termination. Yellow: is copro_agent running?
  • Check state/firmware. Every board publishes its bus, client ID, board name, version and fault bits there once a second; a missing board is missing there. The pre-test bench check B3 lists every expected board and decodes its fault bits (pretest_checks).
  • Two ESC boards with the same ID fight over client 2 or 3. Check SB1 on each.
  • A new board on the bus needs its termination switch set for where it sits in the chain: on only at the two ends.

The same checks from a terminal. The topics are under /talos:

ros2 topic echo /talos/state/firmware      # one message per board per second
ros2 topic echo /talos/state/kill          # true = killed
ros2 topic echo /talos/state/electrical    # POAC rails, once a second
pgrep -af micro_ros_agent                  # copro_agent's process: "can -D can0 ..."
candump can0                               # raw frames on the internal bus (can-utils)
Safety
  • Pull the kill switch before touching anything on the thruster power path: ESC boards, Redcube terminals, thruster cables. MOTOR_PWR is the full battery bus.
  • Bench tests run on a current-limited power supply, not the batteries. LiPo packs can deliver far more current than a short circuit on a board can survive.
  • The ESCs wait about 5 s after power before they spin, and a thruster can start the moment that ends. Keep hands and cables clear of the propellers whenever the robot is enabled.
