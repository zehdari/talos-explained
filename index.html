How Talos works

The Ohio State AUV, explained end to end

Talos is the Underwater Robotics Team's competition submarine. Once its kill switch goes in, it swims the RoboSub course alone. This site explains all of it, from the frame to the code. Tap a part to start there.

Start here · the overview

Everything Talos does is one loop: sensors and cameras measure, software works out where the robot and the props are, a behaviour tree decides what to do, and a controller turns that into thrust 20 times a second. This page walks the loop once, then shows you where to read next.

The short version
  • Talos is UWRT's autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV): 32.4 kg, eight thrusters, two stereo cameras and one Jetson AGX Orin computer. It placed 4th in the RoboSub 2026 Autonomy Challenge.
  • Small boards with RP2040 microcontrollers handle power, the kill switch, the thrusters and the launcher. They talk to the Orin over CAN.
  • On the Orin, about 25 ROS 2 programs do the rest: an EKF for position, a YOLO detector and a map for the props, a behaviour tree for the mission, and an MPC that flies the robot.
  • Nereus, the team's simulator, stands in for the hardware so the same code runs on a laptop. Its viewer is the operator's GUI for the simulator and the real robot alike.
  • New here? Read the whole stack below, then pick a reading path for your sub-team.

Talos in one minute

Talos is an autonomous underwater vehicle. Nobody drives it during a competition run. A diver puts the kill switch in, and from then on everything is decided on board: where to go, what the props are, when to fire.

It is built for one event, RoboSub. The 2026 course had a gate, a slalom, a torpedo board, a table of objects to sort into baskets, bins to drop markers into, magnets to hold against targets, and an octagon to surface in. Talos runs the whole course from one behaviour tree, RepairCompMpcTree. With it the team placed 4th in the 2026 Autonomy Challenge, after 2nd in 2024.

The numbers

Size
925 × 639 × 582 mm without the claw; 32.4 kg dry, on a scale (Hull, frame and layout)
Hull
Two clear tubes in an open aluminium frame: one for the computer, one for the circuit boards (Hull)
Thrusters
8 Blue Robotics T200s in one plane: four push fore and aft, four tilted 45°. ±24 N each, 73 N total (Thrusters and ESCs)
Power
2 × 8000 mAh 5S LiPo (18.5 V, 148 Wh each). The kill switch cuts the thrusters and nothing else (Power and wiring)
Boards
Team-made boards with RP2040 microcontrollers on one CAN FD bus: power and kill (POAC, the power + actuator board), two ESC boards, camera cage, LED, acoustic modem (The circuit boards)
Sensors
VectorNav VN-100 IMU (400 Hz), fibre-optic gyro (about 475 Hz), Nortek DVL1000 (8 Hz), Bar02 depth (20 Hz), a hydrophone (Navigation sensors)
Cameras
2 Stereolabs ZED X Mini, forward and down, 1920 × 1200 at 15 Hz (The cameras)
Tools
A launcher with 2 torpedoes and 2 markers on one servo, a claw, a magnet on a rod (Torpedoes, dropper and claw)
Computer
NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin: ROS 2 Humble over Zenoh, every node of the robot (Computers and networks)
Software
EKF at 30 Hz, YOLO detector at about 10 Hz, a Kalman filter per prop, behaviour trees ticked every 30 ms, an MPC planning 1.5 s ahead every 50 ms (A map of the code)

The whole stack, as one loop

Each box below is one program or one piece of hardware. Data flows up the left side, from the sensors to the mission tree, and commands flow down the right side, from the tree to the thrusters and the launcher. Tap a box for what it does, what it publishes and which chapter explains it.

Three things in this picture explain a lot of how the team works:

  • Every arrow is a message. The programs never call each other. They publish and subscribe to ROS 2 topics, services and actions, so you can watch any arrow from a laptop with ros2 topic echo (ROS 2, the Talos way).
  • The line between "robot" and "simulator" is low. Everything above the drivers doesn't know whether the sensors are real. That is why most work happens in Nereus first, and pool time is for what only water can tell you.
  • Safety lives at the bottom. The kill switch and the boards' own timeouts stop the thrusters even if every program above them is wrong (Commanding Talos).

Follow one decision: firing a torpedo

The best way to see how the pieces fit is to follow one decision through all of them. Here is the first torpedo shot of the run above, from a camera frame to a spring letting go. Step through it; each step links to the chapter that explains it.

In that run the tree turned to the torpedo 28 s before the shot, and that includes the swim there. Much of the rest is waiting on purpose: for mapping to be sure, then for the controller to settle. Each wait is a number someone chose, and the chapters say which file it lives in.

The same chain is also how you debug Talos. When "the torpedo missed", one of these eight steps went wrong, and each one leaves a trace you can record and check on its own (Debugging, bags and data).

How to read this site

The site is written for new team members. You need some programming and high-school physics; everything else is explained where it comes up.

  • Every chapter starts with a short version. If that's all you need today, stop there. The rest is the detail you'll want when you work on that part.
  • Figures are live. Tap or hover boxes, drag sliders, press play. On a phone everything works by tap.
  • Numbers come from the code. When a chapter quotes a rate, a threshold or a weight, it links to the exact file on GitHub, on the branch Talos runs (table below). If the code changes, trust the code.
  • Orange boxes are about safety. They mark anything that can hurt a person or break hardware: the thrusters, the launcher, the batteries, the seals.
  • Commands say where to run them. A tag in the corner of a command block says which machine it is for: your laptop, the Orin or the operator laptop.
  • Unknown word? The Glossary (Appendix B) has about 300 terms, with search and a filter by part. Every topic, service and frame is in Appendix A.
  • Perception and Control are deeper. They began as two separate sites (How Talos sees and How Talos flies), each with its own overview and a page of all its maths.

Which code this site describes

Talos runs riptide_release, which pins every package repo to one commit. Most of them run a feature branch, not master, so check the branch before you clone (A map of the code).
