Thrusters and ESCs

Eight propellers are the only way Talos moves. This chapter follows one thrust command from the controller to a spinning propeller, and shows how to check the thrusters and what goes wrong with them.

The short version
  • Eight Blue Robotics T200 thrusters in one plane: four outer ones push fore and aft, four inner ones at 45° push up or down and sideways at once.
  • The MPC turns each force into a target RPM and sends it over CAN, 20 times a second, to two ESC boards. Each runs an RPM loop that drives four APD 80F3 ESCs over DShot.
  • Thrusters 0, 3, 4 and 7 are marked type: 1 in talos.yaml (presumably the counter-rotating propellers) and the ESC firmware spins them backwards, so a positive command always pushes the same way.
  • The thruster test spins each one at 300 rpm for 3 s. Check that the right one spins and that it blows water the right way.

How the controller chooses the eight forces is in Thrusters and allocation; thruster power is in Power and wiring, the ESC board in The circuit boards and its code in Firmware on the boards.

Eight thrusters, two jobs

All eight sit in one plane across the robot, within 2 cm of the centre of mass fore and aft, two per corner module:

  • The outer four (V…) point along the robot: surge, plus pitch (top against bottom) and yaw (port against starboard).
  • The inner four (H…) are tilted 45°, each pushing up or down and sideways at once: heave, sway and roll.

Names in talos.yaml read Heave/Vector, Upper/Lower, Port/Starboard: HLP is heave, lower, port. The index (0 to 7) is the position in that list, used by the firmware, CAN and the thruster test (ignore the file's comments "thruster 7" and "8" on indices 6 and 7).

  • Every motion uses several thrusters. Sway is the inner four with upper and lower pushing opposite ways vertically, so only the sideways part adds up. So the controller works out all eight at once.
  • Long lever arms. The outer four are about 0.4 m to each side of the centre of mass and 0.24 m above or below it: a small force, a strong torque.
  • One file for the geometry. The MPC's thruster matrix (fossen_model.cpp) and the URDF frames both come from these poses.

From newtons to a spinning propeller

Every 50 ms:

  1. The MPC on the Orin solves for eight forces (±24 N each, 73 N total) and publishes them on thruster_forces.
  2. Force to RPM. forceToRpm inverts the T200 thrust curve (below); the RPMs are logged on command/requested_rpm.
  3. Two CAN frames of four signed 16-bit RPMs (send_thruster_cmd_canbus.c): 0x0B0 to board 0 (thrusters 0 to 3), 0x0F0 to board 1 (4 to 7). No ROS in between.
  4. The ESC board keeps the targets and stops all four thrusters if none arrive for 500 ms. Its RPM loop sends each ESC a throttle over DShot600.
  5. The ESC drives the motor's three phases and replies with its speed, closing the loop.

The boards also take targets on the micro-ROS topic command/thruster_rpm (the force-gauge tool uses it), and only then publish state/thrusters/rpm and /cmd: on the CAN path those stay empty (main.c). The MPC writes to CAN only on wall-clock time with a can0 (configureHardwareOutput), so a simulation can't spin the real thrusters.

Pick a thruster and a force to see the actual numbers at every step.

ESC boards, ESCs and DShot

An ESC (electronic speed controller) turns battery DC into the three phase currents a brushless motor needs. Talos has eight, four on each of two identical boards.

ESC
APD 80F3 (schematic: 15 to 35 V, 80 A), one 15 A fuse each
Board
60 A input fuse; power from the POAC's MOTOR_PWR, which the kill switch cuts
Which thrusters
Board 0: thrusters 0–3, board 1: 4–7, set by a solder jumper; one firmware image fits both

DShot

Instead of a PWM pulse, which needs calibrating and picks up noise, DShot sends a 16-bit digital frame: 11 bits of throttle, a telemetry-request bit and a 4-bit checksum. In bidirectional DShot the ESC answers each frame on the same wire with its electrical revolution time; over the T200's 7 pole pairs that is the motor RPM the loop runs on (dshot_get_rpm). Reversing uses "3D mode" (dshot_update_thrusters):

\text{DShot value} = \begin{cases} 48 + t, & t > 0 \\ 1048 + |t|, & t < 0 \\ 0, & t = 0 \end{cases}

The ESC firmware patch

Stock APD firmware could only reverse in DShot with a "change direction" command and a long pause, and PWM would have lost the RPM reply. So a team member patched 3D mode into APD's firmware (version 2.3.0) with Ghidra (README). The patched binary is not in the repository (APD doesn't publish its firmware): it is in the team's Teams files (navionics channel, "Mk 2 stuff/ESCs") with APD's tools. A replacement ESC needs it and the settings below, loaded through its FLASH CONN header; an unpatched ESC won't reverse.

Why an RPM loop

On the team's force gauge (below) the same throttle gave different thrust on different thrusters and battery levels; the same RPM gave nearly the same thrust (2024 design report). So each board holds an RPM: feed-forward plus PI on the measured RPM, clamped to ±725 of ±999 (Firmware on the boards).

Reversed propellers

A spinning propeller twists the robot the other way; if all eight spun the same way, every surge would also roll Talos. That is the usual reason half carry the counter-rotating propeller. talos.yaml marks them type: 1: thrusters 0, 3, 4 and 7. The ESC firmware spins exactly those backwards (mask 0b1001 on each board, core1_init), so every positive command pushes along the arrow above. The catch: the mask is hard-coded and nothing reads type, so nothing checks they agree (tools/thrusters-hw/build_data.py does, for this page).

Swapping a thruster or propeller

  • Same position, same ESC slot, same propeller hand as the one you take off (look before you remove it). Which hand (CW or CCW) goes on which index isn't written down in the repos.
  • If an index's propeller type must change, change its type in talos.yaml and the mask in core1.c, and flash both ESC boards.
  • Then run the thruster test (below) and check the wash on every thruster.

Thrust, RPM and current

The MPC's force-to-RPM is a fit to Blue Robotics' T200 test-stand data at six voltages (spreadsheet, 2019). Thrust grows with the square of speed:

|F| = K_T\,\rho\,D^4 \left(\frac{n}{60}\right)^2, \qquad K_T = a + b\,n

with n in rpm, \rho = 998.2 kg/m³ and D = 0.076 m. Fitted to every sheet: forward a = 0.3964, b = 1.48×10⁻⁵; reverse a = 0.3114, b = 1.25×10⁻⁵ (fit_thrust_curve.py). The MPC inverts this with Newton's method (forceToRpm).

  • Command RPM, not throttle. 1800 µs gives 33.5 N at 16 V but 38.9 N at 20 V; against RPM the voltage drops out.
  • Reverse is weaker: about 20 % less thrust at the same speed.
  • Current is expensive. It rises roughly with thrust to the power 1.5, so two thrusters at 12 N draw less than one at 24 N.

Measuring thrust: the force gauge

In 2023 the team built a force gauge: a T200 on a lever arm in a tank, with a load cell read by an Arduino (2024 design report, appendix D). thruster_calibration_tools steps one thruster's raw command, waits 5 s per step, and logs command, RPM, current and force to a CSV.

  • It publishes raw values on command/thruster_rpm, so the ESC board must be in raw mode (dshot_tune/set_raw; raises the RAW_MODE fault).
  • The launch file defaults to the old robot Tempest: pass robot:=talos. Five runs from 2023-10-10 are in riptide_mpc/data.

The runs read 70 to 76 % of Blue Robotics' thrust at the same RPM. In October 2026 the MPC dropped the old controller's force-to-RPM curves for the Blue Robotics data: the commit says the old curves had forward and reverse swapped and came from a low-reading gauge (03c8ac1). If you rebuild the gauge, calibrate the load cell with known weights first.

What comes back: telemetry

Every 500 ms each ESC board publishes state/thrusters/telemetry (start_thruster_num 0 or 4):

FieldWhat it tells you
disabled_flagsWhy the thrusters are held at zero (bits in Firmware on the boards). At the pool you will mostly see ESC_POWERON_DELAY (5 s after every kill) and TGT_RPM_STALE (no targets for 500 ms)
vcc_voltageThe ESC supply as the board measures it
esc_telemetry[i].rpmMotor RPM from the ESC's UART packet, in steps of about 14 rpm (100 electrical rpm)
.thruster_readyFalse after 20 missed RPM replies, which also zeroes that thruster
.dshot_offline_cntNon-zero means that thruster was switched off for a while
  • Slow: each ESC reports about 6 times a second, too coarse to see a spin-up.
  • The sign is the motor's: thrusters 0, 3, 4 and 7 show a positive target as negative RPM.
  • The current isn't usable: near 0.04 A in pool runs (Power and wiring). Use the curves above.

The thruster test

thruster_test_canbus (source) spins thrusters 0 to 7 in turn at 300 rpm for 3 s each, until Ctrl-C, which sends zeros. How to run it is in Pool test day. Look for:

  • The right one spins. At Testing Thruster 2..., HUS should turn. Anything else means swapped motor cables or ESCs.
  • It pushes the right way: along its arrow above, blowing water the opposite way. Watch a thread in the wash. Telemetry can't check this.
  • Telemetry matches. Only the ESC under test shows about 300 rpm (negative for 0, 3, 4 and 7); pretest guided checks this.

300 rpm is only about 0.33 N, so Talos won't move on its stand, but a turning propeller can still catch fingers and cables: keep clear. Out of the water, keep runs short.

Before you run it
  • Stop the controller first (control_system.launch.py): the MPC also writes to CAN, and on an enabled robot it spins the thrusters on its own.
  • Robot on its stand, hands, cables and tools clear of every propeller, someone at the kill switch.
  • Pulling the kill switch cuts ESC power even while the program commands; pretest guided checks every thruster stops within 1 s.

What the thrusters do in the pool

The best recording is the untethered run of 2026-10-26, a system-identification run (System identification): six holds (level twice, pitch ±, roll ±), a ramp on each thruster, null-space patterns, speed runs up to 0.7 m/s (the normal limit is 0.6 m/s) and five releases with every thruster off. The figure drops the releases, ramps and null-space patterns, where the thrusters were told what to do, leaving 13.3 of 20.6 minutes.

  • Single thrusters rarely saturate: at 24 N under 0.2 % of the time, only outer ones, only in surge runs.
  • The total limit binds in the fast runs. The sum was at the 73 N cap 8 % of the time (median 25 N), about 70 % of that in the speed runs (sway from 0.35 m/s, surge from 0.5 m/s, heave down at 0.3 m/s) and the rest diving and re-levelling around the releases. The MPC then scales all eight down together (Thrusters and allocation).
  • Holding still is work: on average 2.5 N down and 3.3 N·m nose-down (buoyancy and trim).
  • Time near zero. The inner thrusters sat within ±0.5 N for 10 to 23 % of the time. The ESCs take about 0.8 s to start a motor from rest (2026-10-03 testing); hardware.spin_bias in mpc.yaml can keep every thruster turning with no net force, but is off.
