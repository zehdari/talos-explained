The vehicle · Chapter 3

The circuit boards

The team designs and solders almost every board inside Talos itself. Each one has a single job, most have their own small computer, and those show up in ROS as a handful of topics.

The short version
  • Six team-made boards sit in the two electronics cages, two more on the Orin, and two in each battery housing.
  • Each board that runs code has its own RP2040 microcontroller and talks over CAN, a shared two-wire bus: the internal bus inside the hulls, a slower external one to the batteries. copro_agent on the Orin turns them into ROS topics.
  • The POAC is the hub: batteries in, power rails out, and thruster power only while both the kill switch and its RP2040 allow it.

The boards do the electrical jobs a computer can't: switching tens of amps of thruster power, turning 18 V into 5 V, timing a motor signal to the microsecond. Power and wiring follows the power; Firmware on the boards covers the code.

Every board, in cable order

The internal CAN bus is one cable chain from board to board, in the order of the team's 2026 design report (TDR, Fig. 15). Each board's number is its client ID on the bus.

Each board runs a small micro-ROS client, and copro_agent on the Orin bridges them into ROS under /talos (launch file): state/kill on this page means /talos/state/kill. Thrust skips ROS: mpc_controller writes one raw CAN frame per ESC board per tick, four RPMs each (source). The agent for the external bus is switched off for now (720bdd9), so the battery topics don't reach ROS.

One microcontroller on every board

The older Mark 1 electronics linked chips with I²C, a bus meant for short distances, and in fall 2021 a 3-foot I²C run to the depth sensor failed at a pool test. So Mark 2 gave every board its own RP2040 and joined them with CAN, which is built for long, noisy cables (DeviceList.jsonc). Every board copies one schematic sheet for it (RP2040_with_CAN), so all share one pin map, one CAN driver and one status LED.

The POAC: batteries, rails and the kill switch

The POAC ("power + actuator") replaced the Mark 2 power and actuator boards in 2023-24 and kept the power board's CAN ID 1 (history). It joins the two batteries onto one bus, V+, through ideal-diode switches, so the fuller pack supplies the robot and neither charges the other (Two packs, one bus). It makes the 15 V and 5 V rails and 12 V for the servo, reports them on state/electrical once a second, drives the servo that fires the torpedoes and drops the markers (Torpedoes, dropper and claw), and switches thruster power.

The kill switch, in hardware

The kill switch is the handle on the port bow window; its cable ends at the POAC's J1. It must stop the thrusters even if all software has crashed, so it acts on the power path: three P-channel FETs (transistors used as power switches) sit between V+ and MOTOR_PWR, the line to the ESC boards. They turn on only when two switches in a row agree: the physical switch, and a DG448 analog switch that the POAC's RP2040 opens to kill power "even if the kill switch is inserted" (safety_interface.c). The POAC also publishes state/kill every 150 ms and on every change, and the ESC boards stop their thrusters when they see it. Software kills are in Commanding Talos.

The ESC boards

Each ESC board carries four APD 80F3 ESCs (electronic speed controllers, the power stages that spin brushless motors), fused at 15 A each, fed by the POAC's MOTOR_PWR. The two boards differ only in solder jumper SB1: open makes board 0 (thrusters 0–3, CAN ID 2), bridged makes board 1 (thrusters 4–7, CAN ID 3) (main.c).

The RP2040 talks to each ESC in bidirectional DShot: one wire carries the command out and the RPM back. It keeps the thrusters off for 5 s after power appears and stops them if no command arrives for 500 ms (dshot.h). The silkscreen's ESC 1/5 … ESC 4/8 count from 1, the software from 0 (Thrusters and ESCs).

The camera cage boards

The camera cage breakout (CCBB) takes 15 V, 5 V and internal CAN from the POAC's CAMERA CAGE connector and hands them out in the camera cage:

  • Orin power through a switch. The viewer's "Cycle computer" has the CCBB turn the Orin off for 5 s, then on (ros.c).
  • DVL power on two spare wires of its Ethernet cable.
  • The depth sensor (Blue Robotics Bar02), published as state/depth/raw.
  • Both CAN buses to the Orin HAT, and the external one out through its EXT. CAN jacks to the battery housings.

Two more boards sit on the Orin itself, with no microcontroller: they give it ports it lacks. The Orin HAT on the 40-pin header puts the Orin on both CAN buses (can0 internal, can1 external) and gives the VectorNav IMU a serial port (/dev/ttyTHS1); the FOG board, a card in the M.2 slot, gives the fibre-optic gyro one (/dev/ttyTHS2; Navigation sensors).

The LED and IVC boards

The LED board drives the status light, a separate module with four RGBW LEDs. It takes colour and mode from command/led and flashes red when the kill switch is pulled, blue when it's inserted. Out of the water it runs at 1 % brightness; it dims past 30 °C and turns off at 50 °C (ledc_driver.c).

The IVC board (inter-vehicle communication) sends and hears sound in the water. Its transducer sends messages to Triton, the team's second vehicle; its hydrophone hears Triton and the competition pinger. Each bit is one of two tones, 17 or 19 kHz, for 50 ms in the firmware on master (ivc.c); the 2026 TDR describes 51–53 kHz.

For the pinger (firmware on the unmerged blake/ivc branch), the Orin tells the board which frequency to listen for; Talos listens at two stops and compares how loud it was (Navigation sensors).

Inside the smart battery housings

Each pack sits in a sealed housing outside the hulls with two boards: a BMS board, whose TI BQ40Z80 battery manager balances the five cells and cuts the pack off on a fault, and an MCU board, whose RP2040 drives a small display and reports state/battery on the external bus (What "smart" means). The MCU board's CAN ID is the housing's serial number, and it can cut the robot's power on command (CYCLE_ROBOT, KILL_ROBOT_POWER; ros.c).

When a board misbehaves

  • Look at its status LED first (colours above).
  • Check state/firmware. Every board publishes its name, version and fault bits there once a second, so a missing board is missing there. The pre-test bench check decodes it (pretest_checks).
  • Two ESC boards with the same ID fight over client 2 or 3. Check SB1 on each.
  • Check the TERM ON switches after moving or adding a board. The bus needs a 120 Ω terminator at each end only: in the TDR's chain, the LED board and the Orin HAT.

The same checks from a terminal:

# one message per board per second
ros2 topic echo /talos/state/firmware
# true = killed
ros2 topic echo /talos/state/kill
# POAC rails, once a second
ros2 topic echo /talos/state/electrical
# copro_agent shows as "can -D can0 ..."
pgrep -af micro_ros_agent
# raw internal-bus frames (can-utils)
candump can0
Safety
  • Pull the kill switch before touching the thruster power path: ESC boards, their power terminals, thruster cables. MOTOR_PWR is the full battery voltage.
  • Bench-test boards on a current-limited supply, not the batteries: a LiPo pack can push far more current into a short than a board survives.
  • A thruster can start the moment the ESCs' 5 s power-up wait ends. Keep hands and cables clear of the propellers whenever the robot is enabled.
For board designers: the RP2040 block, schematics, how a board is made, history

The RP2040 + CAN block

MCU
RP2040 (two Cortex-M0+ cores), 12 MHz crystal, W25Q128JV 16 MB flash; builds assume 2 MB. Parts in the block carry an R or C suffix: U1R is the RP2040, U1C the CAN controller.
CAN
MCP2517FD controller on SPI at 20 MHz, TJA1442AT transceiver. The RP2040 makes the controller's 4 MHz clock on GPIO21 from its 48 MHz USB clock (driver).
CAN FD
The internal bus is configured for CAN FD (1 Mbit/s header, 5 Mbit/s data; talos.h), but the firmware has FD switched off ("TODO: Enable CAN FD") and the Orin brings can0 up as classic CAN, so every frame today is classic, at most 8 bytes (CAN and CANmore).
Termination
Slide switch, split 120 Ω (2 × 60.4 Ω); the firmware can read its position.
Debug
Tag-Connect TC2030 pads for SWD, debug serial on GPIO16, status LED on GPIO 13/14/15.

From idea to a board in the robot

  1. Start from the team template in electric_boogaloo, following ProjectCreation.md. Take parts from the repo's libraries and the microcontroller from Device_Sheets/RP2040_with_CAN.SchDot, so the shared firmware just works.
  2. Lay it out for JLCPCB with the repo's 4-layer rules and stack-up. Boards in one stack share an outline: the POAC and ESC boards are both 177.8 × 127 mm.
  3. Design review, then generate the order files (design-rule check, BOM, Gerbers, drill files) and order from JLCPCB. Each round's schematics go into a GitHub release: Tempest MK 2, 2023-24, 2024-25.
  4. Assemble and test. The electrical team solders its own boards, surface-mount parts included.
  5. Bring it up. Flash the first firmware over micro-USB or SWD, add the board's serial number to DeviceList.jsonc, and set the termination switch and any ID jumper. After that the bootloader takes updates over CAN (Flashing and upgrading).

Mark 2 and the POAC

Most of Talos's electronics are older than Talos: the ESC boards, camera cage breakout and battery boards come from the Mark 2 order (January 2023). The POAC, LED board and FOG board came in 2023-24, the IVC board in 2025.

Other designs in the repo

None of these is in the TDR's drawing of Talos's boards: an Acoustics board and preamp (pinger hardware with no firmware project; the pinger code goes through the IVC board), the SBH charger (a bench charger for the battery housings), PoHalf, H-Bridge and Jetson Nano boards for the 2025 second vehicle (LilBoatBoards), and an External Camera HAT, ConnectTech breakout and FPGA acoustics folder whose status is unknown. The IVC board's solder jumper SB5 picks between Talos and that second vehicle; it also carries a Bluetooth audio module that can play sound through the transducer.
